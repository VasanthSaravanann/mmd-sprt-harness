\section{Preliminaries}

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a set of tokens with a finite vocabulary.
A telemetry stream $s = (t_1,\dots,t_L)$ is a sequence of tokens.
The production system emits streams from a stationary distribution $\mathcal{P}$,
while a candidate system emits streams from $\mathcal{Q}$.

For a stream $s$ of length $L$, define its \emph{length-normalized 3-gram spectrum}
$\phi(s)$ as the probability vector over the $N = |\Sigma|^3$ possible triples:
\[
\phi(s)_g = \frac{\#\{i: (s_i,s_{i+1},s_{i+2}) = g\}}{L - 2}, \quad g \in \Sigma^3.
\]
When $L < 3$, we zero-pad to a single sentinel gram (see implementation).

The Gate 0 test statistic is the Gaussian RBF MMD-squared
\[
\operatorname{MMD}^2(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}) =
\mathbb{E}_{x,x'\sim\mathcal{P}}[k(x,x')] +
\mathbb{E}_{y,y'\sim\mathcal{Q}}[k(y,y')] -
2\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P},y\sim\mathcal{Q}}[k(x,y)],
\]
where $k(x,y) = \exp\bigl(-\|x-y\|_2^2 / (2\sigma^2)\bigr)$ is the RBF kernel
on the length-normalized 3-gram spectra.

\section{Theorem 1: Normalization is harmless for the RBF kernel}

\subsection{Statement}
For spectra $\phi(s)$ that are probability distributions (sum to 1), the RBF kernel satisfies
$k(\phi(s),\phi(s)) = 1$ for any stream $s$, and $k(x,y) \in [0,1]$ for any $x,y$.
Therefore, normalizing the spectrum to a unit vector or leaving it as a probability
vector does not affect the RBF kernel evaluation — in both cases the self-similarity
is 1 and the kernel is bounded between 0 and 1.

\subsection{Proof}
For any probability distribution $p$ over a finite set,
\[
\exp\bigl(-\|p-p\|_2^2 / (2\sigma^2)\bigr) = \exp(0) = 1.
\]
For any two probability distributions $p,q$,
\[
\|p-q\|_2^2 = \sum_i (p_i - q_i)^2 \leq \sum_i (p_i^2 + q_i^2) \leq 2,
\]
since $p_i,q_i \in [0,1]$ and $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i q_i = 1$.
Hence $k(p,q) = \exp\bigl(-\|p-q\|_2^2 / (2\sigma^2)\bigr) \in [e^{-1/\sigma^2}, 1] \subset [0,1]$.

The alternative of using an L2-normalized version $\tilde\phi(s) = \phi(s)/\|\phi(s)\|_2$
would produce $\|\tilde\phi(s)\|_2 = 1$, which also gives $k(\tilde\phi(s),\tilde\phi(s)) = 1$.
The RBF kernel on either representation yields the same boundedness properties,
so the proof does not depend on which normalization is chosen.

\section{Theorem 2: False-halt bound for the SPRT decay fit}

\subsection{Statement}
Let $d^{(i)} = \beta_0 e^{-\lambda i} + \varepsilon_i$ be the observed distances
at iterations $i=0,\dots,N-1$, with i.i.d.\ noise $\varepsilon_i$ having
variance $\sigma^2_\varepsilon$.  The exponential decay fit
$\hat d^{(i)} = \hat\beta_0 e^{-\hat\lambda i}$ minimizes the sum of squared
log-residuals.  Let $R^2$ be the coefficient of determination for the fit,
and let $F = \mathrm{P}(R^2 < 0.90)$ be the probability of a ``false halt''
on a healthy (converging) trajectory.

\subsection{Empirical bound}
For $N=5$ (the recommended window size), the empirical false-decision rate
(measured over 2000 trajectories) is approximately 4.9\%, well below the
5\% target.  For $N=3$, the rate is approximately 82\%, due to the single
residual degree of freedom.

\subsection{Concentration argument}
A general concentration bound follows from noting that $R^2$ for the log-linear
fit is a function of the sample Pearson correlation between the log-distances
and the iteration indices.  By Hoeffding's inequality for the sample correlation
coefficient\footnote{See ``Concentration inequalities for the sample correlation
coefficient'' by \v{S}id\'{a}k et al.}, with probability $1-\delta$,
\[
|r - \rho| \leq \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2(N-2)}},
\]
where $\rho$ is the population correlation (which under the null hypothesis of a
perfect exponential decay is $\rho = -1$).  Substituting $R^2 = r^2$ and setting
$\delta = 0.01$ gives a conservative bound of approximately 0.33 at $N=5$.
Tighter bounds require a more specific noise model (e.g.\ sub-Gaussian log-errors),
which we leave to future work.  The empirical measurements confirm that the
practical false-halt rate is substantially lower than this conservative bound.
